Smart Cane

A low-cost walking cane that senses obstacles, guides with sound and vibration, and calls for help automatically if its user falls.

  See how it works   Read the technical report   View on GitHub
Overview

A cane that watches the path ahead

Traditional white canes rely entirely on physical contact and sound reflection to reveal what's nearby. This project augments that experience electronically: two ultrasonic sensors read the space in front of the user at two different heights, a vibration motor and a buzzer translate distance into feel and sound in real time, a small speaker module speaks out warnings as obstacles get closer, and an onboard motion sensor watches for a fall — automatically sending a Wi-Fi alert to a caregiver if the user goes down and doesn't get back up.

Everything runs on a single ESP32 microcontroller, keeping the design affordable to build, easy to modify, and realistic for student, hobbyist, and NGO-scale production.

What's built into the cane

Every sensor and output is mounted along the shaft so the electronics stay compact and the cane still feels and balances like a normal mobility cane.

How It Works

Six systems, working together

Dual-zone obstacle detection

Two ultrasonic sensors mounted at different heights tell a low curb apart from a chest-height obstacle, and scale their response as the user gets closer: 2 m, 1 m, then 40 cm.

Adaptive haptic feedback

A vibration motor in the grip pulses faster and stronger the closer an obstacle gets, giving an instant, silent cue before any sound plays.

Spoken distance alerts

A DFPlayer Mini speaks short warnings at each distance zone, queued so a new warning never cuts off one that's already playing.

Guidance buzzer

An onboard buzzer reinforces the voice and haptic cues near the tip of the cane, helping steer the user around what's ahead once they've been warned.

Fall detection

An MPU6050 motion sensor watches for a hard impact followed by a sustained change in tilt. If that pattern holds for 1.5 seconds, the cane treats it as a confirmed fall.

Emergency Wi-Fi alert

Once a fall is confirmed, the cane plays an emergency voice alert and sends a webhook notification over Wi-Fi, retrying automatically until it succeeds.

Behind the Build

Challenges we ran into

Building a wearable assistive device surfaces problems that don't show up until you're testing on a real sidewalk. A few that shaped this project:

  • Ultrasonic sensors are inconsistent against soft, angled, or reflective surfaces like fabric and glass, so distance thresholds needed real-world tuning, not just bench testing.
  • Telling a genuine fall apart from setting the cane down hard or tapping the tip on a curb took careful calibration of the impact and tilt thresholds.
  • Wi-Fi-based emergency alerts only work when the user is in range of a known network, which doesn't hold once they're out and about.
  • Running two ultrasonic sensors, a motion sensor, an audio module, and Wi-Fi continuously puts real pressure on battery life.
  • Housing exposed sensors and a microSD-based audio module so they survive being tapped on pavement and used outdoors is a mechanical challenge as much as an electronics one.
  • Timing voice, haptic, and buzzer feedback so nothing overlaps or lags behind a person walking at a normal pace required balancing sensor polling against audio playback time.
  • Getting stable serial communication with the DFPlayer Mini is a common early hurdle — power and wiring details matter more than they first appear to.
  • None of this replaces testing with actual blind or low-vision users and orientation-and- mobility instructors, which remains the most valuable feedback loop.
